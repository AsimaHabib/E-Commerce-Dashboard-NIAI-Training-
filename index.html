Every chart starts with a question.

Seven real-world scenarios, from a coffee shop to a startup pitch. Each one is solved by choosing the right chart first, then stating the insight in one sentence.

Coffee shop: which days are busiest?

Chart: Bar chart, highlighted

A small shop wants to schedule staff. Comparing categories calls for a bar chart, and two orange bars make the peak days impossible to miss.

Saturday and Sunday sell up to twice as much as weekdays, so add staff on weekends.

Fitness tracker: did I hit 8,000 steps?

Chart: Line plot with goal line

Steps change over time, so a line fits. A dashed goal line turns "how am I doing?" into a glance.

The goal was missed on Monday, Wednesday, Saturday and Sunday. Weekends are the weak spot.

Streaming report: pie vs bar

Chart: Pie chart vs sorted bar

Six genres with similar values. The pie makes you compare angles; the sorted bar lets you compare lengths from one baseline.

Drama is #1. The bar chart shows it instantly; the pie hides it.

Does studying help?

Chart: Scatter plot with trend line

Two numeric variables call for a scatter plot. Seaborn's regplot adds the trend line.

The relationship is strongly positive: more study hours go with higher scores.

Restaurant manager: when do people tip best?

Chart: Heatmap

Average tip by day and meal time, built from Seaborn's built-in tips dataset. Colour intensity makes the best slot obvious.

Sunday dinner has the highest average tip (about $3.26). Put your best servers there.

Startup pitch: wealth and health over time

Chart: interactive animated Plotly scatter

Built for investors who want to hover and explore. Press play to watch life expectancy and income (log scale) change from 1952 to 2007. Bubble size is population.

Countries drift up and to the right: richer and longer-lived over the decades.

Capstone: e-commerce dashboard

Chart: 2×2 dashboard: line, bar, scatter, pie

One question per panel and one chart type per question: trend, comparison, relationship and share of total.

Saturday is the peak for orders and revenue, and revenue rises with orders. Electronics brings in about 40% of revenue.

What I learned

  • The question points to the chart: trends need lines, comparisons need bars, relationships need scatter plots.
  • Sorted bars beat pies because people judge length along a baseline better than angles.
  • Bar charts must start at zero. Line plots can zoom in, because the slope and the gap to a target matter more.
  • Pie charts are acceptable for part-to-whole with only a few categories and clear differences.
